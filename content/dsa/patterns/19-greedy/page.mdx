<TLDR>
A greedy algorithm builds the answer one choice at a time, always taking the choice that looks best **right now**, and never takes it back. That makes it fast (usually a sort plus one pass, O(n log n) or O(n)) but only correct when you can **prove** the local choice is safe, with an **exchange argument** or by showing the greedy **stays ahead**. When a small counterexample breaks the rule, switch to dynamic programming.
</TLDR>

## When to reach for it

<Callout kind="tip" title="Spot it in the problem statement">

- "Maximum number of…" or "minimum number of…" with a natural rule of thumb: earliest deadline, cheapest so far, furthest reach.
- "Can you reach the end?", "can you complete the circuit?": a single quantity (reach, fuel, balance) summarises the past.
- Scheduling and assignment: meetings, tasks, cookies, boats, where **sorting** first puts the obvious candidate in front.
- n up to 10^5 or more, which rules out trying every combination and hints at O(n log n).

</Callout>

| Variant | The greedy rule | Why it's safe | Classic problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| Running best | keep the best value seen so far (cheapest price, furthest reach) | the past is summarised by one number | best time to buy and sell, jump game |
| Sort, then match | pair the smallest item with the smallest thing it fits | exchange argument | assign cookies, boats to save people |
| Earliest end first | sort by end, keep whatever still fits | greedy stays ahead | non-overlapping intervals, minimum arrows |
| Rounds or frontiers | extend a frontier, count how often you must extend it | stays ahead (BFS levels) | jump game II, partition labels |
| Restart on failure | when a running total goes negative, start again after it | a whole stretch of starts is ruled out at once | gas station, maximum subarray (Kadane) |
| Two passes | satisfy the left-hand constraints, then the right-hand ones | each pass meets a lower bound | candy |

## The core idea: a choice you never regret

Greedy is easy to write and easy to get wrong. The code is rarely the hard part; the hard part is knowing the local choice can't hurt. There are two standard ways to show it, and naming them in an interview is worth a lot.

**Exchange argument.** Take *any* optimal solution. If it doesn't make the greedy choice, swap the greedy choice in and show the result is still valid and no worse. Then some optimal solution agrees with greedy on the first step; repeat for the rest. Assigning cookies is the textbook case: give the **smallest** cookie that works to the **least greedy** child. If a best assignment pairs them differently, swapping the two cookies keeps every child happy, because the bigger cookie satisfies whoever had the smaller one.

<AlgoViz trace={assignCookies} />

**Greedy stays ahead.** Define a measure of progress and show that after every step, greedy's partial answer is at least as good as anyone else's. In interval scheduling, after picking k intervals by earliest end, greedy's k-th interval ends no later than the k-th interval of any other valid schedule, so greedy always has at least as much room left. If greedy is never behind, it can't finish behind.

Both arguments need one thing: the choice must not close doors that an optimal answer needs. When you can't find either argument, look for a counterexample instead, and if you find one, the problem probably needs [dynamic programming](/dsa/patterns/dp-1d).

## Step by step

<Steps>
  <Step title="Write the brute force in one sentence">
    "Try every buy/sell pair", "try every subset of intervals". It tells you what the choices are and what you are optimising.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Propose a rule, usually after sorting">
    Earliest end, smallest that fits, cheapest so far, furthest reach, largest first. Sorting puts the candidate the rule wants at the front of the list.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Attack your own rule">
    Spend a minute on tiny inputs (2–4 items) and try to break it: a long interval that blocks short ones, a coin that's big but awkward. Interviewers expect to see this step.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Prove it, or drop it">
    Make the exchange argument or the stays-ahead argument out loud. If a counterexample survives, switch to DP or search.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Implement the one pass">
    Typically a sort plus a loop that keeps one or two variables. Watch the tie rules (`>=` versus `>`), since that's where greedy solutions fail hidden tests.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Template 1: sort, then match

When items must be paired with slots (cookies with children, people with boats, tasks with workers), sort both and walk them together. A cookie too small for the least greedy child is too small for everyone left, so it can be thrown away; one that fits goes to that child.

## Template 2: earliest end first

To keep the most activities that don't overlap, always keep the one that **ends first** among those that still fit. Picking by earliest start or by shortest length both fail on small examples; earliest end is the rule that stays ahead.

## Template 3: one pass with a running best

Many greedy solutions don't sort at all. They scan once and keep a single number that summarises everything seen so far:

```js
let reach = 0;                                   // Jump Game: furthest index reachable so far
for (let i = 0; i < nums.length; i++) {
  if (i > reach) return false;                   // a gap nothing can cross
  reach = Math.max(reach, i + nums[i]);
}
return true;
```

- <ProblemLink slug="best-time-to-buy-and-sell-stock" /> keeps the cheapest price so far; the best sale today uses it.
- <ProblemLink slug="jump-game" /> keeps the furthest reachable index; reachable indices always form a prefix.
- <ProblemLink slug="jump-game-ii" /> counts how many times the frontier must be extended, which is breadth-first search by levels without a queue.
- <ProblemLink slug="gas-station" /> keeps the fuel since the current start, and restarts after any failure: running dry at station `i` rules out every start before it.
- <ProblemLink slug="partition-labels" /> keeps the furthest last occurrence of any letter in the current piece, and cuts when the scan reaches it.

When one direction isn't enough, use **two passes**. In <ProblemLink slug="candy" />, a left-to-right pass satisfies every "more than my left neighbour" rule and a right-to-left pass the "more than my right neighbour" rules, taking the max so the first pass isn't undone.

## When greedy fails

"Make change with the fewest coins: take the largest coin that fits." With US-style coins that works. With coins `4, 3, 1` and amount 6, greedy takes 4 + 1 + 1, while 3 + 3 needs only two coins. Taking the 4 felt like progress, but it left a remainder that only small coins could fill.

<AlgoViz trace={greedyCoins} />

The fix is to stop committing. Dynamic programming computes the best answer for **every** smaller amount and tries every coin as the last one, so no early choice can trap it.

The same split shows up elsewhere. Fractional knapsack (you may take part of an item) is greedy by value per kilo; 0/1 knapsack (whole items only) is not, and needs DP. Shortest paths with non-negative weights are greedy (Dijkstra); with negative weights they are not.

## Complexity

| Variant | Time | Extra space |
|---|---|---|
| Sort, then one pass (matching, earliest end) | O(n log n) | O(1) to O(n) for the sort or a sorted copy |
| One pass with a running best | O(n) | O(1) |
| Two passes (candy) | O(n) | O(n) for the per-item array |
| Coin change by DP, for comparison | O(amount × coins) | O(amount) |

## JavaScript you'll use

<JsRef to={["sorting", "numbers-math"]} />

```js
const sorted = [...items].sort((a, b) => a - b);   // copy first if the caller's order matters
intervals.sort((a, b) => a[1] - b[1]);             // by end time
let best = -Infinity, cheapest = Infinity;         // sentinels: any real value beats them
best = Math.max(best, price - cheapest);           // Infinity arithmetic is safe: 5 - Infinity is -Infinity
const total = nums.reduce((sum, x) => sum + x, 0); // feasibility totals (gas station)
```

`Array.prototype.sort` sorts **in place** and returns the same array, so `const sorted = arr.sort(…)` also reorders `arr`. That's harmless in a one-off solution, but a bug when the caller (or a later step of your algorithm) relies on the original order. Copy with `[...arr]` or use `arr.toSorted(…)`.

<Callout kind="warning">

- Trusting a greedy rule without trying to break it: a 3-element counterexample costs a minute; a wrong submission costs much more.
- Choosing the wrong sort key: earliest start or shortest length for interval scheduling, instead of earliest end.
- Ties: `start > lastEnd` instead of `>=` rejects intervals that only touch.
- Always taking the biggest step (longest jump, largest coin) when the problem needs the best frontier or a DP.
- Forgetting a global feasibility check, like the total fuel in Gas Station, before trusting the local answer.
- Overwriting instead of taking a max in a second pass, which breaks the constraints the first pass established.

</Callout>

## Interview questions

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="How do you convince an interviewer your greedy algorithm is correct?">
    Name the argument and walk through it on their example. Exchange: "take any optimal answer; if its first choice differs from mine, swapping mine in keeps it valid and no worse." Stays ahead: "after k steps, my measure (finish time, reach, fuel) is at least as good as any other solution's." Then show you tried to break it with a small counterexample. If you can't argue it, say so and offer the DP.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="For which coin systems does greedy change-making work?">
    For "canonical" systems, such as 1, 5, 10, 25 or 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50. Many systems aren't: 1, 3, 4 fails at 6. A practical check is to compare greedy against DP for every amount up to the sum of the two largest coins: a known result says that if greedy is ever wrong, it is already wrong somewhere below that bound. In an interview with arbitrary coins, just use DP.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="Greedy or dynamic programming: how do you decide quickly?">
    Ask whether an early choice can be regretted. If choosing well now never limits later choices (the "greedy choice property") and the rest is a smaller copy of the same problem, greedy works. If a locally good choice can force a worse remainder, as with 0/1 knapsack or coin change with arbitrary coins, you need DP to compare the alternatives. Trying a few tiny inputs against a brute force settles it fast.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="Jump Game II is greedy, but people also call it BFS. Which is it?">
    Both. The indices reachable with k jumps form a contiguous block, which is exactly BFS level k. The greedy scan walks each level once, tracking the furthest index the next level can reach, so it computes BFS distances in O(n) without a queue. The "stays ahead" proof and the BFS proof are the same argument.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>
