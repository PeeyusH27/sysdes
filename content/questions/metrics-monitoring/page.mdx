<TLDR>
Agents push batches through Kafka into a **time-series database** that compresses a sample to about 1.4 bytes. Keep recent data raw and old data in **rollups**, and evaluate alert rules as small state machines with a **for-duration**. The two risks are too many distinct series and noisy pages.
</TLDR>

## Clarifying questions

- **Push or pull?** Agents push here.
- **Resolution and retention?** 10 seconds for how long, then what?
- **Cardinality?** Distinct label combinations set the cost.

## Capacity estimate

| Quantity | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Series | 100K hosts × 100 metrics | **10M** |
| Samples | 10M ÷ 10 s | **1M/s** |
| Raw size | 1M × 16 B × 86,400 s | 1.38 TB/day |
| Compressed | 1M × 1.37 B × 86,400 s | ≈ 118 GB/day |
| Raw, 15 days | 118 GB × 15 | 1.8 TB |
| 1-minute rollups, 90 days | 10M × 1,440 × 6 B × 90 | 7.8 TB |
| 1-hour rollups, 2 years | 10M × 24 × 6 B × 730 | 1.05 TB |
| Dashboard queries | 10K open × 10 panels ÷ 30 s | ≈ 3,300/s |
| Alert queries | 10K rules ÷ 30 s | ≈ 333/s |

About 10.6 TB holds two years of history. The 1.37 bytes a sample is the average reported in Facebook's Gorilla paper; your data will differ. Writes dominate: queries are few and hit small, recent or pre-aggregated data.

## API

| Call | Notes |
|---|---|
| `POST /v1/write` | Batch of `{ labels, samples: [{ t, v }] }`, compressed |
| `GET /v1/query_range` | `query`, `start`, `end`, `step` → series of points |
| `PUT /v1/rules` | Alert rule: query, threshold, `for`, labels, route |
| `GET /v1/alerts` | Pending and firing alerts |

A **series** is one metric name plus one set of labels, such as `cpu{host="web-17", dc="eu"}`. Every distinct label set is a new series.

## Data model

| Store | Holds | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Series index | label to series ids (an inverted index) | `host="web-17"` finds its series fast |
| Head | Newest 2 hours of samples per series, in memory | Backed by a write-ahead log |
| Blocks | Immutable compressed chunks per 2 hours | Timestamps as delta of deltas, values XORed |
| Rollups | min, max, sum, count per minute or hour | Same layout, coarser |

Never put an unbounded value in a label. `user_id` or `request_id` multiplies series by millions and breaks the index, memory and bill.

## Architecture

Samples flow left to right into storage; reads and alerts pull from storage. Kafka in the middle decouples the two speeds. Play **A sample's journey**, **Dashboard query**, **Alert fires** and **Ingest burst**.

## Ingest and storage

Each agent batches about 100 samples every 10 seconds, with a bounded buffer that drops the **oldest** samples if the backend is down. Kafka, partitioned by series, absorbs bursts. Writers append to a write-ahead log and an in-memory head.

Neighbouring samples look alike. Timestamps follow a schedule, so the delta of deltas is nearly always 0: about 1 bit. Values change slowly, so XOR leaves few set bits. A 16-byte sample shrinks to about 1.4 bytes.

## Rollups and queries

Nobody needs 10-second data from last year. Keep raw samples for 15 days, then 1-minute and 1-hour buckets of **min, max, sum, count**: the max shows a spike an average would hide. Queries use the coarsest tier that fits the chart.

Percentiles cannot be averaged. Store a **histogram** per bucket and add histograms to get any window's p99.

## Alerting

An alert rule is a small state machine: **inactive**, **pending**, **firing** once the condition has held for the whole for-duration, then **resolved**. A blip never fires. Missing data counts as bad, so a dead host cannot look healthy. The notifier groups alerts, so 30 hosts make one page.

## Step-by-step answer

<Steps>
<Step title="Define the data">

A sample is a timestamp, a value and a series identity (name plus labels). Everything else follows from series count and sample rate.

</Step>
<Step title="Size it">

10M series every 10 seconds is 1M samples a second: 1.38 TB a day raw, about 118 GB compressed. Say which dominates: writes.

</Step>
<Step title="Make ingestion durable and elastic">

Batching agents with bounded buffers, a stateless gateway, Kafka partitioned by series. Writers can lag or restart without losing data.

</Step>
<Step title="Store for time">

An in-memory head with a write-ahead log, flushed to immutable compressed blocks. Delta-of-delta timestamps and XOR values.

</Step>
<Step title="Control cardinality">

Quotas per team, limits on label values, alerts when series counts jump. Cardinality is the real cost driver.

</Step>
<Step title="Downsample for long retention">

Raw for 15 days, then min, max, sum and count per minute and per hour. Histograms for latency percentiles.

</Step>
<Step title="Alert without noise">

State machine with a for-duration, grouped pages, repeat suppression, silences for maintenance, missing-data rules.

</Step>
<Step title="Monitor the monitor">

A small independent system watches the pipeline: consumer lag, ingest rate, and a canary alert that must always fire.

</Step>
</Steps>

## Failure modes

| What breaks | Effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Backend unreachable | Agents buffer, then drop | Bounded buffer, drop oldest, retry with backoff |
| Burst after an outage | Writers lag | Kafka absorbs; autoscale writers; alert on lag |
| Cardinality explosion | Memory and cost spike | Per-team series quotas; reject unbounded labels |
| Writer or head node crash | Recent data at risk | Write-ahead log replay; replicate the head |
| Alert evaluator dies | Silent failures | Two evaluators; a canary alert that must fire |
| The monitor is down with the system | Blind during an outage | Separate infrastructure and a second basic monitor |

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Gain | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Push | Works for short-lived jobs and NAT | Backend must protect itself from floods |
| Pull | Easy health view and control | Needs service discovery; hard for batch jobs |
| Rollups | Years of history cheaply | Lose raw detail; percentiles need histograms |
| Long for-duration | Fewer false pages | Slower detection |

## Follow-up questions

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="Why Kafka in the middle?">
    Bursts queue instead of dropping, and writers can restart safely.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How do you shard the time-series database?">
    By series hash across writers; queries fan out and merge. Time blocks let old data drop cheaply.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How do you monitor the monitoring system?">
    A small separate stack elsewhere, alerting on lag and a heartbeat canary.
  </FollowUp>
