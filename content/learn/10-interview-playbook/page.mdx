<TLDR>
Use the same structure every time: **requirements, numbers, API and data model, high-level design, two deep dives, trade-offs and a summary**, in about 45 minutes. Say every choice as "X because Y, at the cost of Z", and when stuck, return to requirements and numbers.
</TLDR>

## Why it matters

Knowing the pieces is half the job. A repeatable structure shows seniority.

Interviewers score a rubric: scope, numbers, a design that works end to end, depth where it's risky, trade-offs. A fixed structure hits every row even when you're nervous, and frees your head for the problem.

## The 45-minute plan

Five phases: **5, 5, 15, 15 and 5 minutes**. Press play to fill them in. Checkpoints: scope agreed by minute 5, numbers and API by 10, a working end-to-end design by 25, two deep dives by 40, wrap-up by 45.

<Timeline spec={interviewPlan} />

Behind at a checkpoint? Say so and move on: "I'll keep the data model brief so we have time for the deep dives." Managing the clock out loud is a senior signal.

## The framework as a checklist

Each step leaves something on the board for the next. Play **Getting unstuck** in the diagram to see how to rescue a stalled answer.

<FlowDiagram spec={interviewFramework} />

<Steps>
  <Step title="Requirements">
    Who uses it, for what, and what matters most? Write **functional** requirements (3–5 features), **non-functional** ones **with numbers** (latency, availability, consistency, scale), and **out of scope**. Confirm, then draw.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Estimation">
    Average and peak QPS, read:write ratio, storage over retention. Then say what they **imply**: "4,000 reads/s and 40 writes/s: read-heavy, so caching matters more than write throughput."
  </Step>
  <Step title="API">
    The 3–5 endpoints that matter: methods, key parameters, status codes. Idempotency keys for writes, cursor pagination for lists.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Data model">
    Tables or keys, and the access pattern that picks the store: "every read is a lookup by `code`, so a key-value table keyed by code".
  </Step>
  <Step title="High-level design">
    The simplest boxes and arrows that meet the requirements. Number the flow; walk one request end to end. Everything works before anything is optimised.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Deep dives">
    The two riskiest areas (or where you're steered): algorithm, data structure, numbers, and **what happens when it fails**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Trade-offs and wrap-up">
    What you chose and what it cost, the next bottleneck, what you'd do with more time. Then a 30-second summary.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Frontend variant: RADIO

Frontend rounds (an autocomplete, a news feed client, a chat UI) use the same idea with five stages:

| Letter | Stage | What to cover |
|---|---|---|
| R | Requirements | Features, devices, offline, accessibility, performance targets |
| A | Architecture | Component tree, where state lives, server vs client rendering |
| D | Data model | Client state shape, normalised entities, what's cached or persisted |
| I | Interface | Props and events between components; endpoints, pagination |
| O | Optimizations | Virtualisation, code splitting, network, accessibility, i18n, security |

## What interviewers look for

Every level gets the same question. The bar is what moves.

| Signal | Mid | Senior | Staff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Requirements | Asks some questions | Drives scope; non-functional numbers | Reframes the problem; finds hidden requirements |
| Design | A working design | Clean components, data flow, scaling | Evolution path; org and operational concerns |
| Deep dive | One area | Two areas, with failure modes | The riskiest area, quantified |
| Trade-offs | Names some | Justifies each, and when to switch | Cost, complexity, team impact |

## Talking through trade-offs

Interviewers score reasoning, not a perfect answer. Two templates cover almost every decision:

- "I'll use **X** because **Y**. The cost is **Z**. If **W** changes, I'd switch to **V**."
- "We could do **A** or **B**. A is simpler, B scales further. At our 4K QPS, A is enough for now."

Filled in from the case studies:

- "I'll return a **302** because we promised click analytics. The cost: every click reaches our servers. Drop analytics and I'd switch to a 301."
- "**One counter** or **range allocation**? One is simpler; ranges remove the hot spot. At 40 writes/s either works, and ranges are a few lines more."
- "I'll put SMS on **separate priority queues** so an OTP never waits behind a campaign. The cost: more queues and workers. For transactional-only traffic, one queue per channel would do."

## Drawing conventions

Your board is how the interviewer follows your thinking.

- **Consistent shapes**: one each for services, data stores and queues.
- **Label every arrow** with what flows (`GET /:code`, "click event", "replication").
- **Number the request flow** (1, 2, 3…), so you both say "step 4" instead of pointing.
- **Assumptions in a corner box**: numbers you'll refer back to (4K QPS, 100:1 reads, 7-character codes).
- Request path left to right, data stores at the bottom or right, as in this site's diagrams.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="warning">
- Jumping to tools ("Kafka, Cassandra, Kubernetes") before the requirements.
- Over-engineering: sharding and multi-region for 40 writes/s.
- Ignoring failure: every box can die, every network call can time out.
</Callout>

Prepare three lines:

- **Pushback**: "Good point. If that matters, I'd change…" It's a hint, not an attack.
- **A knowledge gap**: "I don't know X in detail, but here's how I'd reason about it…" First principles beat bluffing.
- **Getting stuck**: back to requirements and numbers, simplify (one region, one server), then scale step by step out loud.

## Practice plan

Work in this order, aloud and on a timer: [URL shortener](/learn/case-url-shortener), [rate limiter](/questions/rate-limiter), [notification system](/learn/case-notification-system), [chat](/questions/chat), [news feed](/questions/news-feed), then payments.

- **Spaced repetition.** Redo each after **3, 7 and 21 days**: the second attempt shows what you remember, the third makes it stick.
- **Record yourself.** Playback exposes silences, filler words and skipped numbers.
- **Practise with the [playground](/playground/url-shortener)**: build on a canvas and get graded before seeing the reference.
- **Score every run** 1–4 per rubric dimension. Watch the trend, not single scores.

| Dimension | 1: needs work | 4: strong |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements | Starts drawing at once | Scope and numbers agreed by minute 5 |
| Estimation | No numbers | QPS, storage, and what they imply |
| API and data model | Vague endpoints | Clear endpoints, keys, access patterns |
| High-level design | Boxes, no flow | A numbered flow, end to end |
| Deep dives | Stays on the surface | Two areas in depth |
| Failure modes | Not mentioned | What breaks, how it's detected and recovers |
| Trade-offs | Choices stated as facts | Each choice's cost, and when to switch |
| Communication | Draws in silence | Thinks aloud, checks in, manages the clock |

## Interview questions

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="How do you handle an ambiguous problem?">
    Ask clarifying questions, write down functional and non-functional requirements, state assumptions, and confirm before designing. The ambiguity is deliberate: they want to see you narrow scope.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="The interviewer pushes back on your choice. What do you do?">
    Treat it as a hint: restate the concern, weigh it against the requirements, then defend with numbers or adapt explicitly.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How deep should you go?">
    Breadth first until the design works end to end; then deep where risk is highest, or where you're steered.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="What if you get stuck?">
    Back to requirements and numbers, simplify, then scale step by step out loud.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>
