<TLDR>
Something is always failing, so contain it: a **timeout** on every call, jittered **backoff** for safe retries, and **circuit breakers** with fallbacks. Set **SLOs** and alert on what users feel, not CPU.
</TLDR>

## Why it matters

Chain 10 services at 99.9% each and only about 99% of requests succeed. At scale something is always broken. So contain failures, show something useful anyway, and notice before your users do.

Play **Failure spike**, then **Half-open probe**: the breaker trips, a fallback answers, then it heals.

## SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and error budgets

<Doodle name="monitoring" size={150} caption />

"Reliable enough" needs a number. Measure something users feel (an **SLI**), like requests answered in under 300 ms. Set a target (an **SLO**), like 99.9% over 30 days. The customer contract, the SLA, is looser.

The gap is your **error budget**. At 99.9% and 10M requests a month, 10,000 may fail. Budget left: ship faster. Budget spent: freeze risky changes.

| Availability | Downtime per 30 days |
|---|---|
| 99% | 7.2 h |
| 99.9% | 43.2 min |
| 99.99% | 4.32 min |

Each extra nine cuts downtime 10 times and costs far more. One 45-minute outage uses a whole 99.9% month. At 99.99% you get about 4 minutes, too fast for a human, so failover must be automatic.

## Timeouts, retries and jitter

<Doodle name="backoff" size={150} caption />

A call that hangs holds a thread until the caller runs dry. So every call gets a **timeout**, set near the dependency's slow case (80 ms normally, so about 250 ms).

A retry fixes a blip: one lost packet, and the second try works. It hurts when the service is overloaded: 1,000 requests a second with 3 tries each can become 3,000. So be careful:

- Retry only calls that are safe to repeat (a card charge is not one).
- Wait a random, growing time (**jitter**). Otherwise clients that failed together retry together.
- Retry in one layer only. 3 layers with 3 tries each send **27 calls** at a struggling service.

Switch **Retry policy** to **Exponential + full jitter** and watch the peak after recovery drop.

## Circuit breakers and bulkheads

A drowning service needs you to stop calling it. A **circuit breaker** is a fuse: it trips, calls fail fast, and a fallback answers.

- **Closed**: calls pass; failures are counted.
- **Open**: after 5 failures, calls fail instantly for about 10 s.
- **Half-open**: one trial call. Success closes it; failure reopens it.

A **bulkhead** gives each dependency its own pool, like a ship's watertight rooms. If recommendations hangs, checkout still has its slots.

Raise **Dependency error rate** until the breaker trips, then lower it.

## Load shedding and prioritisation

Overloaded, serving everyone means serving no one. Reject the excess fast.

- **Shed early and cheaply**, before parsing or database work. Reply `503` with `Retry-After` (`429` for one client over its limit).
- **Drop abandoned work**: a request queued past its deadline has no one waiting.
- **Shed from the bottom**: keep checkout and login; drop prefetch, analytics and batch jobs first.
- Per-client limits: [rate limiter](/questions/rate-limiter).

## Graceful degradation

Decide each page's fallback before the outage, not during it.

| When this is down | Show instead |
|---|---|
| Recommendations | Cached bestsellers |
| Reviews | Nothing; the page still sells |
| Search | Cached popular queries |
| Database writes | Read-only mode with a banner |

**Kill switches** (feature flags) turn off extras under load. Test fallbacks: one that never runs quietly breaks.

## Redundancy and failover

Two of everything helps only if they cannot fail together. Two app servers on one database, zone or deploy are not redundant.

- **N+1.** One spare covers a failure or a rolling deploy. Across 3 zones, losing one must still leave full capacity, so run each zone below about 66% busy (the two survivors then carry everything).
- **Zones vs regions.** Zones are milliseconds apart and replicate synchronously. Regions survive a disaster but replicate async.
- **Active-passive vs active-active.** An idle standby fails over slower. Serving everywhere is fast but must handle conflicting writes ([Step 6](/learn/consistency-cap)).
- **RPO** is how much data you can lose. **RTO** is how long you can be down.

| Setup | RPO | RTO |
|---|---|---|
| Nightly backups | Up to 24 h | Hours |
| Async replica, other region | Seconds to minutes | Tens of minutes |
| Sync replica, other zone | 0 | 1 to 2 min |
| Active-active regions | Near 0 | Near 0 |

A payments ledger needs RPO 0. A blog can live with 24 h.

## Safe deploys

Most outages start with a change. Keep changes small, visible and reversible.

- **Canary**: send 1% of traffic to the new version, compare errors, then widen. At 10,000 requests a second, 1% is 100 a second: enough to spot a broken deploy in minutes, while 99% of users never see it.
- **Blue/green**: new beside old; switch at the load balancer, and back instantly.
- **Feature flags**: deploy dark, enable slowly, turn off without a deploy.
- **Automatic rollback** when the canary burns error budget faster than the old version.

## Observability

At 3 a.m. you need three answers, fast:

| Signal | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metrics | Is something wrong? | Error ratio, p99 latency |
| Logs | What happened in this request? | JSON with `reqId`, status |
| Traces | Where did the time go? | API 320 ms, database 250 ms |

- For services, watch rate, errors and duration (use percentiles, not averages).
- A trace header carries one id across services, so ten services join into one waterfall.
- **Alert on symptoms and burn rate, not CPU.** On a 99.9% SLO, 1.44% errors spends 2% of the month's budget in an hour: page. A slow burn over three days: ticket.

## Incident basics

The pager fires. Stop the bleeding first.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Detect">
    A burn-rate alert pages on-call.

  <Step title="Triage and communicate">

  </Step>
  <Step title="Mitigate first">
    Roll back, fail over, shed load or flip a flag. Root cause can wait.

  <Step title="Resolve and learn">

## Interview questions

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="Why add jitter to retries?">
    Clients would retry in lockstep and knock a recovering service back down.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="SLO vs SLA?">
    The SLO is your internal target, and its error budget sets how fast you ship. The SLA is the looser contract with penalties.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How do you survive a zone failure?">
    3 zones with spare capacity, and synchronous database replication with automatic failover.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="What do you alert on?">
    Burn rate on what users feel (errors, latency against the SLO). CPU and disk go on dashboards.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How can retries make an outage worse?">
    Retries at every layer multiply load (3 × 3 = 27 calls). Use jittered backoff, breakers and one retrying layer.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>
