<TLDR>
Something is always failing, so contain it: a **timeout** on every call, jittered **backoff** for idempotent retries (one layer, within a budget), and **circuit breakers** and bulkheads with fallbacks. Set **SLOs** with error budgets and alert on what users feel, not CPU.
</TLDR>

## Why it matters

Chain 10 services at 99.9% each and only about 99% of requests succeed. At scale something is always broken, so contain failures, degrade gracefully, and notice before your users do.

Play **Failure spike**, then **Half-open probe**: the breaker trips, a fallback answers, then it heals.

## SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and error budgets

"Reliable enough" needs a number.

- **SLI** (indicator): a measured ratio, like requests that succeed in under 300 ms.
- **SLO** (objective): the internal target, like 99.9% over 30 days.
- **SLA** (agreement): the customer contract with penalties (usually service credits), looser than the SLO.
- **Error budget** = 100% − SLO: at 99.9% and 10M requests a month, 10,000 may fail. Budget left, ship faster; budget spent, freeze risky changes.

| Availability | Downtime per 30 days | Per day |
|---|---|---|
| 99% ("two nines") | 7.2 h | 14.4 min |
| 99.9% | 43.2 min | 1.44 min |
| 99.99% | 4.32 min | 8.64 s |
| 99.999% | 25.9 s | 0.86 s |

Each nine cuts downtime 10× and costs far more. At 99.99%, about 4 minutes a month won't even cover getting paged and logging in: four nines need automated detection and failover. The chart shows the yearly allowance.

## Timeouts, retries and jitter

A hung call holds a thread, a connection and memory until the caller runs dry.

- **Timeouts.** Always. Set from the dependency's p99 (80 ms → about 250 ms); pass a **deadline** downstream so inner calls quit with the caller (gRPC deadlines, `AbortSignal.timeout(ms)` on `fetch`).
- **Backoff with full jitter.** Wait a random 0 to `base × 2^attempt`; without jitter, clients that failed together retry together.
- **Retry only idempotent calls**: `GET`, `PUT`, `DELETE`, or `POST` with an idempotency key. Never a 400 or validation error.
- **Retry amplification.** 3 layers × 3 attempts = **27 calls** on a struggling service. Retry at one layer, nearest the failure.
- **Retry budgets.** Cap retries near 10% of requests: an outage adds 10% load, not 3×.

## Circuit breakers and bulkheads

A drowning dependency needs you to stop calling. A **circuit breaker** fails fast; a **bulkhead** gives each dependency its own pool, so one can't starve the rest.

- **Closed**: calls pass; consecutive failures (or the windowed error rate) are counted.
- **Open**: after 5 failures, calls fail instantly for a cooldown (say 10 s) and a fallback answers. Threads stay free; the dependency gets room.
- **Half-open**: exactly one trial call. Success closes; failure reopens for another cooldown.

Bulkheads are a ship's watertight compartments: 20 concurrent calls for payments, 10 for recommendations. If recommendations hangs, checkout still has its 20. Same idea for database pools: the [connection pooling deep dive](/deep-dives/connections-pooling).

Raise **Dependency error rate** until the breaker trips; lower it and watch one probe close it.

## Load shedding and prioritisation

Overloaded, serving everyone means serving no one. Reject the excess fast; serve the rest well.

- **Shed early and cheaply**: at the load balancer or on arrival, before parsing, auth or database work. Reply `503` with `Retry-After` (`429` for one client over its limit).
- **Drop abandoned work**: a request queued past its deadline has no one waiting.
- **Prioritise**: critical (checkout, login), normal (browsing), sheddable (prefetch, analytics beacons, batch jobs). Shed from the bottom.
- **Limit concurrency, not just rate**: an adaptive limit (like TCP congestion control) tracks a shifting request mix better than a fixed requests-per-second cap. Per-client limits: [rate limiter](/questions/rate-limiter).

## Graceful degradation

Decide each page's fallback before the outage, not during it.

| When this is down | Degrade to |
|---|---|
| Recommendations | Bestsellers from a cached list |
| Reviews service | Hide reviews; the product page still sells |
| Search | Cached popular queries, category browsing |
| Payment provider | Second provider, or charge later if risk allows |
| Primary database writes | Read-only mode with a banner |

**Kill switches** (feature flags) turn off extras like personalisation or live counters under load. Test fallbacks: one that never runs quietly breaks.

## Redundancy and failover

Two of everything helps only if they can't fail together: two app servers sharing a database, zone or bad deploy aren't redundant.

- **N+1 and N+2.** One spare covers a failure or rolling deploy; two cover a failure during maintenance. Per zone too: if peak needs 6 across 3 zones, losing one must still leave 6, so run 3 per zone (9, or 150%).
- **Zones vs regions.** Zones, milliseconds apart, replicate synchronously: the high-availability default. Regions, hundreds of kilometres apart, survive regional disasters but replicate async, since cross-region writes are slow.
- **Active-passive vs active-active.** An idle standby is simpler but slower to fail over; serving everywhere fails over almost instantly but must handle conflicting writes ([Step 6](/learn/consistency-cap)).
- **RPO** (recovery point objective): how much data you can lose. **RTO** (recovery time objective): how long you can be down.

| Setup | RPO (data lost) | RTO (time down) | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nightly backups, manual restore | Up to 24 h | Hours | Low |
| Async replica in another region, manual failover | Seconds to minutes | Tens of minutes | Medium |
| Sync replica in another zone, auto failover | 0 | 1–2 min | Medium |
| Active-active across regions | Near 0 | Near 0 | High |

A payments ledger needs RPO 0 and an RTO of minutes; a marketing blog can live with RPO 24 h. In the calculator, add serial dependencies and watch availability fall; add a redundant pair and watch it climb.

## Safe deploys

Most outages start with a change: a deploy, a config push, a migration. Keep changes small, observable, reversible.

- **Canary**: 1% of traffic (or one zone), compare errors and latency with the old version, then 10%, 50%, 100%.
- **Blue/green**: new beside old, switch at the load balancer, switch back instantly.
- **Feature flags**: deploy dark, enable for staff, 1% of users, everyone; turn off without a deploy.
- **Automatic rollback** when the canary burns error budget faster than the baseline.
- **Backward-compatible migrations** (expand, migrate, contract), so both versions run side by side.

## Observability

At 3 a.m. you need three answers, fast:

| Signal | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metrics | Is something wrong, and how much? | Request rate, error ratio, p99 latency |
| Logs | What happened in this one request? | Structured JSON: `reqId`, route, status, duration |
| Traces | Where did the time go across services? | API 320 ms, of which the database took 250 ms |

- **RED for services**: Rate, Errors, Duration (percentiles, never just the average). **USE for resources**: Utilisation, Saturation, Errors (CPUs, disks, pools, queues).
- **Tracing with OpenTelemetry**: the W3C `traceparent` header carries trace context, so ten services join into one waterfall. Sample for cost; keep every error and slow trace.
- **Alert on symptoms and burn rate, not CPU.** On a 99.9% SLO, burn rate 14.4 (1.44% errors) spends 2% of the month's budget in an hour: page. Rate 6 over six hours spends 5%: page. Rate 1 over three days spends 10%: ticket. Pair each long window with a short one (1 hour with 5 minutes) so alerts fire and clear fast.

## Incident basics

The pager fires. Stop the bleeding first.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Detect">
    A burn-rate alert pages on-call, ideally before customers notice.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Triage and communicate">
    Name an incident commander, open a channel, update the status page. One coordinates; others investigate.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Mitigate first">
    Roll back, fail over, shed load or flip a flag. Root cause can wait.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Resolve and learn">
    A **blameless postmortem**: timeline, contributing causes, what went well, action items with owners. Blame hides information.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Every alert links to a **runbook**: meaning, dashboards, first safe steps. Page only for urgent, actionable problems; fix or delete noisy alerts.

## Interview questions

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="Why add jitter to retries?">
    Without it, clients retry in lockstep and knock a recovering service back down. Full jitter (a random wait up to the exponential cap) spreads them best.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="SLO vs SLA?">
    The SLO is your internal target (99.9% under 300 ms), and its error budget sets how fast you ship. The SLA is the looser contract with penalties.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How do you survive a zone failure?">
    3 zones with N+1 capacity, no zone-local state, and sync database replication with automatic failover. Drain a zone on purpose to prove it.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="What do you alert on?">
    Burn rate on what users feel (errors, latency against the SLO) over a short and a long window. CPU and disk go on dashboards, unless a disk is 95% full.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How can retries make an outage worse?">
    Synchronised retries at every layer multiply load on a struggling service (3 × 3 = 27 calls). Use jittered backoff, a retry budget, breakers and one retrying layer.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>
