<TLDR>
Nouns become classes, verbs become methods, and every method gets one question: who owns this? Keep each rule next to the data it reads, prefer parts you swap over family trees, and stop adding classes when a new one protects nothing.
</TLDR>

## What an LLD round scores

The interviewer is watching how you **decide**, not whether your code compiles in their head. Four things earn points:

- **Requirements:** you ask about limits and scope before you draw.
- **The model:** sensible nouns, honest relationships.
- **Change:** you name the requirement most likely to change and show the one place it lands.
- **A working core:** one scenario, end to end, with edge cases said out loud.

This lesson follows a lending library, from one class that does everything to classes that each own one thing.

<Callout kind="interview" title="What interviewers listen for">
"Who owns this?" and "if this rule changed, which class would I edit?" Naming classes is cheap. Justifying the boundaries is the score.
</Callout>

## Nouns and verbs from a prompt

Highlight the nouns (classes) and the verbs (methods).

> A library lends books to members. A member may hold three books at a time, each for 14 days. Returning late costs 0.50 per day.

| In the prompt | Becomes | Why |
|---|---|---|
| library | `Library` | The entry point |
| books, members | `Book`, `Member` | Each has data and rules |
| lends, returns | `borrow`, `giveBack` | Verbs are methods |
| holds a book for 14 days | `Loan` | A link with its own data is a noun |

A noun with no data to protect and no behaviour is just a field. A `day` or a `fee` is a number.

## Who owns the behaviour

In `god-library-before.js` one class does everything. Then students get 7 days instead of 14. The 14 lives in two places, so the email and the fee disagree. That is a **god class**: many reasons to change.

The fix is moving each rule next to the data it reads. In `library-after.js` a `Loan` knows its due day, a `Member` knows their limit, and `Library` only coordinates. Students are one new `Member`.

## Composition over inheritance

Think of a toy car with a swappable battery pack. The car **has** a battery. That is **composition**. Inheritance says "is a", and breaks fast: an `ElectricCar` is a `Car` until `fillUp` calls `refuel()` on it.

In `inheritance-before.js` every new trait multiplies the tree: electric, hybrid, truck, electric truck. In `composition-after.js` a vehicle has an energy source, and a hybrid is just a new part.

## What each line means in code

Each line is a question about **lifetime**.

| Relationship | Ask | In the code |
|---|---|---|
| Association | Does A know B? | A field or argument holding B |
| Aggregation | A holds B, but B outlives A | A field filled from outside |
| Composition | A creates and owns B | `new B` inside A |
| Dependency | Does A use B for one call? | A parameter or a local |

## Encapsulation and tell, don't ask

In `library-after.js`, `member.loans.push(loan)` reaches into a member and decides for it. That is **asking**, and the limit rule now lives in whoever calls.

`reach-in-before.js` shows the cost: the desk checks the limit, the kiosk forgets, and one member holds two books on a limit of one. In `tell-after.js` the member hides its list and exposes `borrow`, so no caller can skip the rule.

## The too-many-classes trap

A class earns its place when it has **state to protect**, **behaviour that would be duplicated**, or **a requirement that varies there**. Otherwise it is a function or a field.

```text
LoanFeeCalculatorFactory -> LoanFeeCalculator -> FeeResult
BookManager, MemberManager, LoanManager, LibraryManager
```

Nothing there protects anything. Compare `Loan.feeAt(day)`: one method, on the one class that knows the due day. Five to eight classes usually suffice, as in the [parking lot question](/questions/parking-lot).

<Callout kind="warning" title="Over-modelling costs points too">
Cannot say what a class protects in one sentence? Merge it into its caller.
</Callout>

## Follow-up questions

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="How do you decide what becomes a class?">
    It has data to protect or behaviour that would be duplicated. A link with its own data, like a loan, counts. The rest are fields.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="When is inheritance the right choice?">
    When every subclass can stand in for the parent and the tree stays shallow. If you override a method to throw, use a part instead.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>
